# Sisyphus Optimizing in a Dynamic Universe
## Detailed concept notes for the talk

> Working theme: a general framework for how we answer questions under limited information — from meeting a person, to joint Rubin–Euclid astrometry, to foundation-model representations such as JAISP.

---

## 1. Central idea

The talk is not fundamentally about machine learning, astronomy, Fisher information, or optimization by themselves.

It is about a more general loop:

1. We have a **question**.
2. The question determines **what matters**.
3. What matters determines a **metric / loss / notion of distance**.
4. We have some **data / observations**.
5. We cannot usually carry all raw observations into every decision, so we form a **representation / compression / model**.
6. We perform **inference / optimization**.
7. We get an **answer** with some remaining uncertainty.
8. We ask whether the answer is **good enough / sufficiently constraining**.
9. If not, there are three fundamentally different possibilities:
   - we are using the wrong question / metric / loss;
   - the needed information is already in the data but our compression discarded or obscured it;
   - the information is genuinely absent and we need new / better observations.
10. Then we repeat.

This loop should work for:

- “Should I eat lunch now or in an hour?”
- “What can I infer about a person I just met?”
- “Where is this source in a common Rubin–Euclid frame?”
- “What is dark energy?”
- “Should I change my partner?”
- and, eventually, “Can one learned representation support many different future scientific questions?”

The core statement:

> **Information is always information about a question.**

A second core statement:

> **A new question does not necessarily require new data. It may require a new compression, representation, metric, or readout of the same data.**

And the joint-processing version:

> **Compress the shared reality, not each instrument separately.**

---

# 2. The master flowchart

## 2.1 Question

- **What am I trying to answer?**
- **What do I care about?**
- What latent quantity / state / decision is relevant?

The question defines which parts of reality are relevant and which are nuisance.

### Human example
**Question:** “I just met this person. What can I infer about who they are?”

Possible subquestions:
- Are they interesting?
- Are they trustworthy?
- Would I enjoy spending time with them?
- Are they reliable?
- What should I expect from them?

The underlying object is not directly observed. We observe behavior and context and infer something latent.

### Astrometry example
**Question:** “Given Rubin and Euclid observations, where is this source?”

Latent quantity:

\[
\theta = (x,y)
\]

The true source position is not directly handed to us. We infer it through pixel-level measurements, PSFs, noise, sampling, calibration, and learned or classical estimators.

---

## 2.2 What matters?

The question defines what counts as relevant information.

That leads to:

- **objective**
- **utility**
- **loss**
- **metric**
- **distance**

### Human language
“Do I like them?” and “Can I trust them?” require different evidence and different internal scoring functions.

A conversation about football may be highly informative for:
- whether conversation flows easily;
- shared interests;
- humor.

The same conversation may be almost useless for:
- reliability;
- financial trust;
- behavior under pressure.

### Astrometry language
For source position, the relevant information is spatial:
- source morphology;
- PSF;
- centroid shifts;
- subpixel information;
- noise;
- resolution;
- WCS calibration.

The correct objective must reward preservation of the information needed for localization.

---

# 3. Loss / metric / distance

The metric defines what “closer to the answer” means.

This is the geometry of the hill.

> **Change the ruler, change the landscape.**

Optimization is movement through that landscape.

### Human example
A person can look “close to ideal” under one metric and very far away under another.

Possible dimensions:
- fun;
- kindness;
- reliability;
- compatibility;
- ambition;
- emotional safety;
- intellectual interest.

The choice of metric is not delivered by the data. It comes from the question and values.

### Astrometry example
The objective might encode:
- positional residual;
- negative log-likelihood;
- uncertainty-weighted offset;
- spatial reconstruction fidelity.

The loss can fail even when optimization converges.

A model may numerically converge while the scientific answer remains weakly constrained.

Therefore:

> **Convergence of the optimizer is not convergence of inquiry.**

---

# 4. Data / observations

We do not interact with the latent state directly. We interact with observations generated by it.

### Human example

Data:
- words;
- tone;
- expressions;
- actions;
- promises;
- context;
- how they treat other people;
- how they behave under stress;
- what others report;
- repeated encounters.

Nuisance / noise / biases:
- mood;
- social setting;
- self-presentation;
- strategic behavior;
- observer bias;
- stereotypes;
- memory;
- projection;
- limited sampling of contexts.

The observer is not passive. Interacting with someone can change their behavior.

So a more realistic human model is something like:

\[
p(D \mid \theta, \text{context}, \text{observer}, \text{action})
\]

and perhaps even:

\[
\theta = \theta(t,\text{context},\text{relationship})
\]

The “latent person” is not necessarily static.

### Astrometry example

Data:
- Rubin pixels;
- Euclid pixels;
- multiple bands;
- PSFs;
- noise maps;
- sampling;
- WCS;
- image morphology.

Nuisance / systematics:
- photon noise;
- blending;
- wavelength-dependent morphology;
- PSF variation;
- resampling;
- calibration;
- detector / instrumental effects;
- source morphology;
- background.

Unlike the human case, source position is approximately fixed over the relevant observation interval, so the latent parameter is cleaner.

---

# 5. Model

A model connects observations to the latent thing we care about.

The model is itself a simplification.

### Human example

A mental model of another person might include:
- traits;
- intentions;
- reliability;
- expectations;
- behavioral patterns;
- context dependence.

This can be thought of as informal Bayesian updating:
- prior beliefs;
- new observations;
- revised beliefs.

But we should avoid claiming the brain literally runs Bayesian inference or a neural architecture identical to JAISP.

The point is structural:
- observations;
- internal representation;
- prediction;
- update.

### Astrometry example

A more explicit generative / inferential structure:

\[
p(D \mid \theta)
\]

with:

\[
\theta=(x,y)
\]

or, in learned form:

\[
D \rightarrow z \rightarrow \hat{\theta}
\]

where \(z\) is a learned representation and a task head reads out position.

---

# 6. Compression / representation

This is one of the central ideas of the talk.

We cannot carry every raw observation into every future decision.

So we compress.

\[
D \rightarrow T(D)
\]

where \(T(D)\) may be:
- a statistic;
- a catalog entry;
- a centroid;
- a latent vector;
- a posterior;
- a mental summary;
- an embedding.

## 6.1 Lossless versus lossy

### Lossless compression
The full original data can, in principle, be reconstructed.

### Lossy compression
Some information is discarded.

But scientifically, the more useful distinction is:

> **A representation may be lossy with respect to the full data but effectively lossless with respect to a particular question.**

If \(T(D)\) preserves all information relevant to parameter \(\theta\), then conceptually:

\[
I(T(D);\theta) = I(D;\theta)
\]

This is related to the sufficient-statistic idea.

But a representation sufficient for \(\theta\) may be insufficient for another parameter \(\phi\):

\[
I(T(D);\phi) < I(D;\phi)
\]

So:

> **Different questions may require different compressions of the same data.**

---

## 6.2 Human example

Raw experience:

\[
\text{hundreds of observations}
\]

becomes:

- “nice”
- “interesting”
- “trustworthy”
- “unpredictable”
- “safe”
- “not for me”

These are extremely lossy summaries.

But “nice” may be enough for:

> “Would I enjoy another coffee with this person?”

It may be hopelessly insufficient for:

> “Would I trust this person with something important?”

The data may already contain relevant clues, but the previous compression may have thrown them away.

So a new question may require:
- going back to remembered observations;
- reweighting evidence;
- constructing a different mental representation.

No new data are necessarily required.

---

## 6.3 Astrometry example

Raw pixels can be compressed into:
- a classical centroid;
- a catalog position;
- stem features;
- a fused latent;
- a task-specific position estimate.

A catalog-level compression may throw away cross-instrument pixel information.

Pixel-level or representation-level joint processing can preserve more of what is relevant before task-specific compression occurs.

---

# 7. Inference / optimization

Given:

- question;
- objective / metric;
- data;
- representation / compression;
- model;

we produce an answer.

### Human
Our mental model is updated.

### Astrometry
A posterior or learned estimator yields a position and uncertainty.

The output is not just a number. It should also encode how uncertain the answer is.

---

# 8. Entropy / uncertainty

Entropy sits around the state of knowledge.

In an inference context:

- broad posterior → high uncertainty;
- narrow posterior → low uncertainty.

Conceptually:

> **Entropy tells us how uncertain we still are.**

But:

> **Low entropy is not automatically truth. We can be confidently wrong.**

This matters in both examples.

### Human
A highly confident first impression can be wrong.

Low subjective uncertainty may reflect:
- bias;
- overconfidence;
- inadequate model class;
- poor prior assumptions.

### Astrometry
A narrow uncertainty estimate can still be wrong if:
- calibration is biased;
- systematics are omitted;
- the likelihood is misspecified;
- the learned uncertainty is miscalibrated.

---

# 9. Surprise

Surprise for an observation can be thought of as:

\[
-\log p(D)
\]

A surprising event is one our current model thought unlikely.

### Human
A quiet, restrained person suddenly behaves in a way completely inconsistent with our current model.

### Astrometry
An observation lands far away from what our current source / calibration / model predicts.

But:

> **Surprise is not the same as useful information.**

A bizarre event may be surprising while telling us very little about the parameter we care about.

---

# 10. KL divergence / information gain

What we often care about is not merely:

> “Was this observation surprising?”

but:

> **“How much did this observation change what I believe about the thing I care about?”**

Information gain can be represented by:

\[
D_{\rm KL}
\left[
p(\theta\mid D)
\,\Vert\,
p(\theta)
\right]
\]

A clean three-way distinction:

> **Entropy:** how uncertain am I?  
> **Surprise:** how unexpected was this observation?  
> **KL / information gain:** how much did I learn?

This is a useful memorable line for the talk.

---

# 11. Fisher information

Fisher provides a local view of information.

Conceptually:

> **If the true parameter moved slightly, how strongly would the observable distribution change?**

High Fisher:
- observations are highly sensitive to the parameter;
- the likelihood is locally steep;
- the parameter can be tightly constrained.

Low Fisher:
- flat direction;
- degeneracy;
- weak sensitivity.

So in the mountain metaphor:

> **Fisher tells us the local shape / steepness of the hill.**

---

## 11.1 Human example

Keep Fisher mostly qualitative here.

If the target question is:

> “Is this person reliable?”

then:
- favorite color → almost no information;
- football preference → probably little information;
- whether they consistently keep commitments → much more information.

Different interactions have different sensitivity to the latent trait we care about.

This is closer to expected information gain than a literal Fisher calculation, but the conceptual parallel is useful.

---

## 11.2 Astrometry example

Now Fisher becomes explicit.

Let:

\[
\theta=(x,y)
\]

For an approximately Gaussian positional likelihood:

\[
F
\sim
\begin{pmatrix}
1/\sigma_x^2 & \cdots\\
\cdots & 1/\sigma_y^2
\end{pmatrix}
\]

So better positional precision corresponds to more Fisher information.

At a simple heuristic level:

\[
F \propto \frac{1}{\sigma_{\rm pos}^2}
\]

This makes astrometry a particularly clean first scientific example.

---

# 12. Fisher inside JAISP

An especially interesting future direction is to ask:

> **How much information about a scientific parameter survives each stage of the learned compression?**

For astrometry:

\[
D_{\rm pixels}
\rightarrow
z_{\rm stems}
\rightarrow
z_{\rm fused}
\rightarrow
\hat{x},\hat{y}
\]

For each representation \(z\), one can ask how much Fisher information about \((x,y)\) remains.

Conceptually:

\[
F_{\rm pixels}
\rightarrow
F_{\rm stems}
\rightarrow
F_{\rm fused}
\rightarrow
F_{\rm head}
\]

If:

\[
F_{\rm after} < F_{\rm before}
\]

then the compression discarded information relevant to astrometry.

If:

\[
F_{\rm fused} \approx F_{\rm pixels}
\]

then the fused representation is close to lossless **with respect to astrometry**, even though it is not globally lossless.

A possible local-Gaussian estimate:

\[
F_z = J_z^T C_z^{-1}J_z
\]

where:

\[
J_z =
\left[
\frac{\partial \langle z\rangle}{\partial x},
\frac{\partial \langle z\rangle}{\partial y}
\right]
\]

and \(C_z\) is the covariance of the representation under observational noise.

A practical route is to use source injection:
- inject sources at known positions;
- perturb by small \(\pm\delta x,\pm\delta y\);
- generate repeated noise realizations;
- measure response in:
  - raw pixels;
  - VIS stem;
  - Rubin stem;
  - fused latent;
  - final astrometry head.

Then compare information retention.

This could quantify the idea:

> **A foundation representation can be characterized by how much information it preserves about different scientific questions.**

Later the same analysis could be repeated for:
- photometry;
- shape;
- redshift;
- detection-related parameters.

---

# 13. Is the answer good enough?

After inference:

> **Is the answer sufficiently constraining for the current question?**

Two main branches.

---

## 13.1 Yes — good enough for the current question

Then:
- use the answer;
- stop;
- or ask a new question.

A new question can imply:
- new objective;
- new loss;
- new metric;
- new compression;
- new task head.

But critically:

> **New question does not imply new data.**

And, eventually:

> **Die on that hill :D**

---

## 13.2 No — not constraining enough

Now diagnose the failure.

There are three fundamentally different possibilities.

### A. Wrong / inadequate question, objective, metric, or loss

Maybe the ruler is wrong.

Actions:
- ask a better question;
- redefine utility;
- change loss;
- change metric;
- change distance;
- alter which information is considered relevant.

This changes the landscape itself.

---

### B. The information exists in the current data, but the representation / compression is inadequate

The answer may be bad because useful information was:
- discarded;
- averaged away;
- compressed too aggressively;
- hidden in a representation inappropriate for the task.

Actions:
- recompress;
- build a different representation;
- use a different statistic;
- use another readout / task head;
- return closer to the raw data.

No new observation is required.

This is the branch most directly connected to foundation models.

---

### C. The required information genuinely does not exist in the current observations

Then we need new / better data.

Possible changes:
- more observations;
- greater depth;
- higher spatial resolution;
- larger area / coverage;
- better S/N;
- different wavelength;
- new modality;
- different physical observer distance;
- different vantage point;
- different instrument;
- another epoch.

This leads to experimental design.

---

# 14. Experimental design

Do not merely ask:

> “How do I get more data?”

Ask:

> **“Which observation is expected to teach me the most about the thing I care about?”**

Formally, choose design \(d\) to maximize expected information gain:

\[
\mathrm{EIG}(d)
=
\mathbb E_y
\left[
D_{\rm KL}
\left(
p(\theta\mid y,d)
\Vert
p(\theta)
\right)
\right]
\]

Equivalently, maximize expected reduction in posterior entropy.

Under local / Gaussian assumptions this connects naturally to maximizing Fisher information.

---

## 14.1 Human experimental design

Suppose the question is:

> “Can I rely on this person?”

Another hour of small talk may give very little new information.

A context involving:
- responsibility;
- a commitment;
- disagreement;
- stress;
- cooperation;

may have much higher information gain.

The observation should be chosen for the question.

Important nuance:
- social “experiments” are interactive;
- the act of probing changes the interaction;
- ethical and relational constraints matter.

---

## 14.2 Astrometry experimental design

If the question is source position, possible observation changes include:
- more photons;
- sharper PSF;
- better sampling;
- additional high-resolution band;
- another epoch;
- improved calibration;
- another instrument.

A small amount of high-resolution information may be more valuable than a huge number of low-resolution photons.

---

# 15. Joint processing

This is the major bridge from the general information framework to Rubin–Euclid and then JAISP.

The key idea:

> **One experiment may contain information about a quantity that another experiment measures poorly.**

So one dataset can **lend information** to another.

---

## 15.1 Human parallel: multiple contexts

We observe the same person in:
- work;
- friendship;
- stress;
- family;
- sport;
- conflict;
- relaxed conversation.

These are heterogeneous observational contexts.

Each context:
- has different noise;
- reveals different dimensions;
- has different selection effects.

The deeper idea is not to separately reduce each context to a simplistic label and then compare labels.

Instead:

\[
D_1,D_2,D_3,\ldots
\rightarrow
\text{shared latent model of the person}
\]

Again, this is a structural analogy, not a claim that cognition literally implements the same architecture.

---

## 15.2 Rubin–Euclid

Separate processing:

\[
D_R \rightarrow C_R
\]

\[
D_E \rightarrow C_E
\]

then:

\[
C_R + C_E
\]

By the time catalogs are combined, each instrument has already performed its own lossy compression.

Joint processing instead aims for:

\[
(D_R,D_E)
\rightarrow
Z_{\rm joint}
\rightarrow
\text{measurement}
\]

before the cross-instrument information is discarded.

This motivates the line:

> **Compress the shared reality, not each instrument separately.**

---

# 16. Joint inference versus information transfer

Useful distinction:

## Joint inference

Both instruments independently carry information about the same parameter.

If conditionally independent given \(\theta\):

\[
F_{\rm joint}
=
F_R + F_E
\]

in the classical Fisher picture.

---

## Information transfer

One modality contains a strength that can improve inference in another.

For Rubin–Euclid astrometry:
- Euclid VIS provides sharp spatial information;
- Rubin provides different wavelength / depth / contextual information;
- the shared representation can make VIS localization useful to measurements associated with other bands.

This is richer than simply averaging two estimates.

---

## Information completion

A learned multimodal representation can infer structure weakly observed in one channel from correlated structure present in another.

Masked-band reconstruction naturally encourages this.

---

# 17. Foundation representation

Repeatedly asking different questions raises a new problem.

If every new question requires:
- starting from raw data;
- constructing a new compression;
- learning a new representation;

then the cost is enormous.

So ask:

> **Can we learn a representation rich enough to survive changes in the question?**

The goal is not absolute losslessness.

It is closer to:

> **A broadly useful, minimally task-specific lossy compression.**

or:

> **A representation that tries to preserve information useful for many future questions, including some not explicitly optimized during pretraining.**

---

# 18. Human parallel for foundation representations

Humans do not usually keep entirely separate mental databases for:
- “person for football”;
- “person for advice”;
- “person for trust”;
- “person for work”;
- “person for friendship.”

We try to form a richer internal representation of the person and interrogate it differently depending on the question.

A future question may expose that the representation was too compressed, biased, or incomplete.

Then:
- re-evaluate;
- re-weight;
- gather new information.

This parallels the foundation-model problem at an abstract level.

---

# 19. JAISP as the payoff

The JAISP idea becomes:

\[
\text{Rubin + Euclid pixels}
\rightarrow
\text{shared learned representation}
\rightarrow
\text{different scientific heads}
\]

Possible heads:
- detection;
- astrometry;
- photometry;
- shape;
- redshift.

The same representation is reused rather than rebuilding the entire processing chain for every question.

This is where the talk transitions from philosophy / statistics / cognition into the concrete research program.

The framing:

> **Instead of building a new compression from scratch for every scientific question, learn a rich representation once and ask different questions of it later.**

---

# 20. JAISP and astrometry as the first concrete example

The astrometry question:

> “Where is the source?”

JAISP can use joint Rubin–Euclid information and exploit the sharper Euclid VIS localization.

The position head can be thought of as one particular readout of the shared representation.

Then the Fisher question becomes:

> **How much astrometric information about \((x,y)\) is present in each level of the representation?**

This turns “the latent looks useful” into a scientific information-retention question.

Potential comparisons:
- Rubin pixels alone;
- Euclid VIS pixels;
- Rubin stems;
- VIS stem;
- fused latent;
- fused + VIS readout;
- final position estimate.

Possible qualitative expectation:

\[
F_{\rm Rubin}
<
F_{\rm fused}
<
F_{\rm fused+VIS}
\sim
F_{\rm VIS}
\]

if the joint representation successfully transfers high-resolution spatial information.

This is only a hypothesis until measured.

---

# 21. Dynamic systems and why the human example is more difficult

The human example should not be presented as identical to astrometry.

It is deliberately more difficult.

The source position is approximately a stable latent parameter.

A person:
- changes;
- reacts;
- learns;
- performs;
- adapts to the observer;
- behaves differently across contexts.

Therefore the “landscape” itself can move.

This supports the title:

> **Sisyphus Optimizing in a Dynamic Universe**

Sometimes the optimizer is not merely climbing a difficult hill.

The hill itself changes.

---

# 22. “Die on the hill” and “Die on the slope”

Two endings for a question.

## Die on the hill

We reached an answer good enough for the question.

Then:
- stop;
- or ask another question.

The summit is local to the question.

---

## Die on the slope

The answer continues improving, but:
- uncertainty remains;
- the theory is incomplete;
- the experiment is unfinished;
- the scientific lifetime ends before the summit is reached.

This is the more melancholy scientific condition.

The Weinberg connection belongs here.

The search for a deeper / final description can be experienced:

> **half in longing, half in mourning**

because the desired final theory may remain beyond one lifetime.

The point is not simply “science is sad.”

It is that finite observers operate inside open-ended inquiry.

---

# 23. Sisyphus

At the beginning:

- the optimizer pushes the rock;
- optimization looks like punishment;
- every objective is another hill.

Throughout the talk:
- loss defines the hill;
- Fisher describes local curvature;
- entropy describes uncertainty;
- surprise tells us when reality violates expectations;
- KL tells us how much the new observation changes belief;
- compression determines what information survives;
- new experiments change the available information;
- new questions redefine the hill.

By the end, repetition acquires a different meaning.

The rock comes back because:
- questions change;
- metrics change;
- representations lose information;
- new observations become possible;
- systems themselves change.

This is not necessarily failure.

It is inquiry.

---

# 24. Final line

The final slide should contain:

> **One must imagine Sisyphus happy.**

This should come only after the audience has seen why the rock keeps returning.

The ending meaning:

- there is no universal final loss function;
- no single compression is sufficient for every possible question;
- no observer possesses all relevant information;
- every answer creates possible new questions;
- the purpose was never simply to stop pushing.

Possible spoken lead-in:

> “Maybe the problem was never that the rock comes back.  
> Maybe the rock comes back because we keep finding better questions.”

Then:

> **One must imagine Sisyphus happy.**

# 28. Important conceptual cautions

These are worth keeping in speaker notes even if they never appear onscreen.

## Do not equate entropy with ignorance in every context
Entropy is a property of a probability distribution. High entropy can be the correct representation of genuine uncertainty.

## Do not say “maximize entropy” for experimental design
The target is generally:
- maximize expected information gain;
- maximize expected reduction in posterior entropy;
- maximize expected KL;
- or, under appropriate approximations, maximize a Fisher criterion.

## Surprise is not information gain
An unlikely datum can be surprising without being especially informative about the target parameter.

## Fisher is local
It characterizes local sensitivity around a parameter value and is most naturally connected to Gaussian / regular likelihood approximations.

## Do not claim the brain literally performs Bayesian inference
Use the human example as a structural analogy:
- evidence;
- prior expectations;
- compression;
- update;
- decision.

## Human latent states are dynamic
A person is not a fixed parameter vector. The interaction changes both observer and observed.

## Foundation-model representations are not lossless
JAISP should not be described as preserving all information.
The stronger and safer statement is:
- it aims to preserve broadly reusable information;
- whether it is effectively lossless for a particular scientific question is testable.

## Joint processing is not simply concatenation
The key value is preserving and transferring complementary information before independent task-specific compressions destroy it.

# 29. Potential deeper thesis

The talk may ultimately be saying something stronger:

> **Inference is the art of deciding what information to keep, what information to seek, and what notion of distance makes an answer meaningful.**

A foundation model is one response to the fact that we often do not know all future questions in advance.

JAISP becomes an astronomical implementation of that philosophy:

> learn a representation of the shared Rubin–Euclid sky that can later be interrogated by multiple scientific questions.

And Fisher provides one possible quantitative test:

> how much information relevant to each question survived the compression?
